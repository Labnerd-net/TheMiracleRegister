Synopsis: Healing of J. Kent Lenahan from Traumatic Injuries

Saint:        John Neumann
Miracle:      Healing of J. Kent Lenahan from Traumatic Injuries
Recipient:    J. Kent Lenahan Jr.
Condition:    Crushed skull, broken collarbone, lung punctured by a rib, internal bleeding — from being crushed against a utility pole
Date:         July 8, 1949 (accident); recovery over the following ~5 weeks
Location:     Wayne, Pennsylvania (site of the accident; Lenahan's hometown was neighboring Villanova); Bryn Mawr Hospital, Bryn Mawr, PA (treatment)

---

On the evening of July 8, 1949, nineteen-year-old J. Kent Lenahan Jr., of Villanova, Pennsylvania, was riding on the running board of a moving car near Wayne — a common enough practice for the era, and one that had carried countless young men home from countless summer evenings without incident. This time the car swerved out of control and crushed Lenahan against a utility pole.

He arrived at Bryn Mawr Hospital in a condition that the attending physicians had rarely seen survive transport, let alone treatment. His skull was crushed, one eye nearly torn from its socket. His collarbone was broken. A fractured rib had punctured one of his lungs. He was bleeding from his ears, his nose, and his mouth, and had lapsed into a coma. His temperature had spiked to 107°F and his pulse was racing at 160 beats per minute — vital signs incompatible, by any contemporary medical standard, with survival for more than a few hours. He lingered between life and death for four days while doctors, having concluded there was nothing more medicine could do, effectively stopped treatment; the family was left to wait for the outcome everyone in the room expected.

It was at this point that Lenahan's mother obtained a small piece of cloth cut from a cassock that had belonged to Bishop John Neumann, dead eighty-nine years by then but already the subject of a growing devotional following in Philadelphia, where his body still lay beneath the altar of St. Peter the Apostle Church, and laid it on her unconscious son. According to the accounts of those present, his condition began to turn within hours: his temperature fell from 107°F to 100°F, and his racing pulse slowed to nearly normal. Over the following days he regained consciousness and began a recovery that defied everything the treating physicians had told the family to expect. Less than five weeks after being crushed against a utility pole with a fractured skull and a punctured lung, Lenahan walked out of Bryn Mawr Hospital in excellent health. He went on to work as a music teacher and band leader.

The case did not go unquestioned even within the family's own community — there was, by several accounts, a longstanding local debate over whether Lenahan's recovery should be credited to Neumann's intercession or simply to what one source dryly calls his "youthful constitution," the resilience sometimes seen in young trauma patients that can produce recoveries doctors don't expect. That debate is worth preserving here for balance, since the surviving secondary sources raise it themselves. It was not, however, the conclusion the Vatican's Sacred Congregation of Rites reached after formal investigation: reviewing the hospital records and the sequence of events surrounding the application of the relic, the Congregation approved Lenahan's case in March 1963 as a miracle attributable to Neumann's intercession, alongside the peritonitis cure of Eva Benassi in Italy. Together, the two cases satisfied the two-miracle requirement then in force for beatification, and Pope Paul VI beatified John Neumann in Rome on October 13, 1963.
