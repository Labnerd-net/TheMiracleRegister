Synopsis: Healing of a Woman from Leukemia

Saint:        Marguerite d'Youville
Miracle:      Healing of a Woman from Leukemia
Recipient:    Known in the medical literature only by the initials "L.N." - full name not publicly confirmed
Condition:    Acute myeloblastic (myelogenous) leukemia, with relapse and an inexplicably prolonged second remission
Date:         Late 1970s onset; reviewed by the Vatican in 1987; this was the miracle used for the December 9, 1990 canonization
Location:     Montreal, Quebec, Canada

---

A young Roman Catholic woman, identified in the surviving medical literature only by the initials "L.N.," was diagnosed with acute myeloblastic leukemia, a form of the disease physicians describe as among the most aggressive known, with a typical survival window of about 18 months even with treatment. She was treated with chemotherapy and went into remission, then relapsed, a common and usually fatal course for this diagnosis. Her family and fellow parishioners, along with members of the Grey Nuns of Montreal (the Sisters of Charity founded by Marguerite d'Youville), began praying for her recovery through d'Youville's intercession - L.N. is described in secondary accounts as connected to the Sisters of Charity community, though the precise nature of that connection is not consistently detailed across sources. Following the prayers, L.N. went into a second remission.

What drew Vatican attention was not the remission itself but its duration. In April 1987, Dr. Jeanne Drouin, a Montreal physician involved in the case, brought more than 400 bone marrow slides spanning roughly eighteen months of L.N.'s treatment to Dr. Jacalyn Duffin, a hematologist and medical historian at Queen's University in Ontario. Duffin was not told the purpose of the review and examined the slides "blind," confirming the original diagnosis and the relapse before being surprised to learn, partway through, that the patient was still alive and well years after the samples were taken - far longer than medical literature would predict for a second remission following relapse in this disease. Duffin later wrote that she found no scientific explanation for the remission's unusual length and permanence.

Duffin's slide analysis and testimony were submitted to the Congregation for the Causes of Saints (now the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints) in Rome as medical evidence in d'Youville's canonization cause. A panel representing the Congregation traveled to Canada to take her testimony directly. Duffin, who has described herself as an atheist, testified only to what she could confirm medically: that she could offer no scientific account for the outcome. She was explicit that Vatican officials did not ask her to declare the case a miracle, only whether science could explain it. The Vatican's own approved description of the case, reflecting this medical caution, was that this was a "miraculously prolonged second remission" rather than a sudden or inexplicable disappearance of disease - the healing was gradual and mediated through ordinary treatment, with the miraculous element located in the remission's unprecedented and medically unaccountable durability.

The case was accepted as the miracle required for d'Youville's canonization. Pope John Paul II canonized her on December 9, 1990, making her the first person born in Canada to be declared a saint. The Vatican's own published biography for the canonization names this leukemia cure as the miracle behind the cause, though - consistent with the Vatican's general practice of not identifying miracle recipients by name - it does not give the patient's identity.

The experience had a lasting effect on Duffin's own career: she went on to study hundreds of Vatican miracle investigations dating back to 1588 and published a full-length history of the Church's medical vetting process, "Medical Miracles: Doctors, Saints, and Healing in the Modern World" (Oxford University Press, 2009), describing herself afterward as "an atheist who believes in miracles" in the narrow sense that she could not explain this outcome scientifically.

Open questions noted below affect precision on the exact year of onset and the patient's full identity; both are treated in this synopsis as unresolved rather than guessed.
