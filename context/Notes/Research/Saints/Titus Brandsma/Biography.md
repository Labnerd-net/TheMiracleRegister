Biography: Titus Brandsma

Born Anno Sjoerd Brandsma on February 23, 1881, at the Oegeklooster family dairy farm near Bolsward, in the Dutch province of Friesland, he was the second-youngest of six children in a devout farming family — five of the six siblings eventually entered religious life. At seventeen he joined the Carmelite order at Boxmeer, taking his father's name, Titus, as his religious name; he made his first profession in 1899 and was ordained a priest on June 17, 1905. Sent to Rome for further study, he earned a doctorate in philosophy from the Gregorian University in 1909 and became a lifelong scholar of Carmelite mysticism and a translator of St. Teresa of Ávila.

In 1923 Brandsma helped found the Catholic University of Nijmegen, where he taught philosophy and the history of mysticism and served as rector magnificus in 1932–33. Alongside his academic work he was deeply engaged in journalism: he edited a regional newspaper and, from 1935, served as ecclesiastical adviser to roughly thirty Dutch Catholic newspapers and spiritual director to the Union of Catholic Journalists, pressing for professional standards in the Catholic press. When the German occupation ordered Dutch newspapers to print Nazi propaganda and anti-Jewish material, Brandsma, acting on the Dutch bishops' behalf, spent ten days in January 1942 personally visiting editors across the country to urge them to refuse. The Gestapo arrested him in Nijmegen on January 19, 1942. After months of imprisonment and interrogation in the Netherlands and Germany, he was transferred to Dachau concentration camp in June 1942, where forced labor and illness led to his admission, on July 18, to the camp hospital block — notorious for experimentation on prisoners. He was killed there by a lethal injection of phenol on July 26, 1942, reportedly forgiving and giving his rosary to the nurse who administered it; he was cremated, so no bodily relics survive.

Pope John Paul II beatified him as a martyr on November 3, 1985. Pope Francis canonized him on May 15, 2022, after the Vatican recognized a healing attributed to his intercession. He is widely, though not yet officially, regarded as a patron of journalists and the Catholic press.
