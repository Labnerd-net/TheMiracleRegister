Biography: Josephine Bakhita

Josephine Bakhita was born around 1869 in the village of Olgossa in the Darfur region of Sudan, into a Daju family. She never learned her birth name: around age seven to nine she was kidnapped by Arab slave traders while out in the fields, and the trauma of the abduction caused her to forget it entirely. Her captors gave her the name "Bakhita," Arabic for "fortunate" — an irony she later embraced rather than rejected. Over the following decade she was sold through a succession of owners in Sudan, enduring beatings and a horrific ritual scarification in which one master cut some 114 patterns into her skin and rubbed salt into the wounds to keep the scars raised. In 1883 she was purchased in Khartoum by the Italian Vice Consul Callisto Legnani, who treated her with unaccustomed kindness and, two years later, brought her with him to Italy. There she passed into the household of the Michieli family of Zianigo, serving as nanny to their daughter Mimmina, who boarded with the Canossian Sisters in Venice. Through the sisters Bakhita encountered Christianity for the first time, and when the Michielis returned to reclaim her for a posting in Sudan, she refused to go. The ensuing dispute reached an Italian court, which ruled in November 1889 that because slavery had never been legally recognized in Italy — and had been outlawed in Sudan before her birth — Bakhita had in fact been free from the moment she set foot on Italian soil. She was baptized Josephine on January 9, 1890, and entered the Canossian Daughters of Charity, professing vows in 1896. From 1902 she lived in Schio, near Vicenza, working as cook, seamstress, sacristan, and doorkeeper for over four decades; the townspeople affectionately called her "Madre Moretta," the dark-skinned mother. She died of pneumonia on February 8, 1947. Pope John Paul II beatified her on May 17, 1992, and canonized her on October 1, 2000.
