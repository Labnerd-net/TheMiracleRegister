**IMPORTANT NOTE ON WHICH MIRACLE THIS IS:** The project's "Priority Additions" list describes Romero as having "1 miracle (beatification)." Research for this file did not find evidence of that framing. As a martyr, Romero was fully dispensed from the beatification miracle requirement — no miracle was needed or used for his May 23, 2015 beatification. The one documented, Vatican-adjudicated miracle in his file is the **canonization** miracle: a healing decreed by Pope Francis on March 6, 2018, more than two and a half years *after* the beatification, clearing the way for the October 14, 2018 canonization. This file documents that canonization miracle. The "(beatification)" label on the priority list appears to be a documentation error that should be corrected when this saint is entered into the DB — flagged explicitly here and in the Data.md dispensation note.

In 2015, Cecilia Maribel Flores de Rivas, a married mother in El Salvador expecting her third child, developed a high-risk pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia. On September 4, 2015 (date as reported by several Salvadoran and wire outlets; not independently confirmed by a primary document), she underwent an emergency cesarean section at a maternity hospital in San Salvador and delivered her son, Luis Carlos. In the hours that followed, she developed HELLP syndrome — a severe, rapidly progressing complication of pregnancy marked by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and a dangerously low platelet count — on top of the preeclampsia. Her kidneys and lungs began to fail, and she was transferred to the general hospital of the Instituto Salvadoreño del Seguro Social (ISSS), where physicians placed her in an induced coma. Reporting on the case states her kidney function had collapsed to roughly 2 percent of normal capacity. Physicians treating her reportedly told her husband, Alejandro Rivas, that they held little hope for her survival and that prayer was the only recourse left; one physician is quoted (in Spanish-language press, translated) telling him to pray "if you believe in God… ask Him for your wife to be saved with a miracle."

That night, Rivas found a prayer card bearing an image of the recently beatified Archbishop Óscar Romero — accounts differ on whether it had been tucked into the family Bible or fell out of it — and knelt to pray for Romero's intercession on his wife's behalf. According to his later account, Flores began showing the first signs of improvement at around 2:00–2:30 a.m., the same approximate time he had been praying. Over the following days her condition reversed dramatically: press accounts describe her kidney function recovering from roughly 2 percent to 70 percent of capacity within about five days, and she was discharged from the hospital on September 10, 2015 — in her husband's words, a turnaround "from being in a coma on September 4 to discharge on September 10" that he and the family regarded as inexplicable by medical means alone. She made what is described as a full and lasting recovery, with no reported lasting organ damage despite the severity of the multi-organ failure she had experienced.

The Archdiocese of San Salvador, acting through the postulation of Romero's canonization cause, documented the case and forwarded it to the Vatican's Dicastery (then Congregation) for the Causes of Saints; one Salvadoran press account dates the archdiocese's formal submission of documentation to March 1, 2017. The case underwent the Church's standard medical and theological review for a canonization miracle: a panel of physicians (the Consulta Medica) examines whether the recovery can be explained by known medical means, and — only if the doctors find no scientific explanation — a panel of theologians then evaluates whether the healing can be attributed to the intercession of the candidate for sainthood specifically. Salvadoran press reporting on the case states that the reviewing doctors "no encontraron argumentos científicos en su cura" ("found no scientific arguments for her cure"), though this is a secondhand paraphrase from Catholic press rather than a quotation from the Vatican's own findings — the Consulta Medica's actual deliberations are not published (see Open Questions).

On March 6, 2018, Pope Francis received Cardinal Angelo Amato, then-Prefect of the Congregation for the Causes of Saints, in audience and authorized the Congregation to promulgate decrees recognizing several miracles, including the one attributed to Blessed Óscar Romero's intercession. The official Vatican press bulletin, published the following day, records only that "il miracolo" was "attribuito all'intercessione del Beato Oscar Arnolfo Romero Galdámez, Arcivescovo di San Salvador, Martire" ("the miracle attributed to the intercession of Blessed Oscar Arnulfo Romero Galdámez, Archbishop of San Salvador, Martyr") — consistent with the standard pattern across these decrees, it names neither the recipient nor any clinical detail. Secondary press coverage characterized the Vatican's finding as recognizing the recovery as "scientifically inexplicable in the light of present-day knowledge," though this precise phrasing was not found in the primary bulletin text itself and should be treated as a journalistic gloss on the standard decree language rather than a verified direct quotation (see Open Questions). The decree cleared the final procedural requirement for Romero's canonization, which Pope Francis celebrated on October 14, 2018, together with the canonization of Pope Paul VI. Flores and her husband traveled to Rome for the ceremony; she has spoken publicly and on the record about the case in multiple interviews, crediting Romero's intercession for her survival and attributing her son's life to it as well.

Open questions before this can move past stub status:
- **Beatification-vs-canonization labeling.** The project's priority-list note calls this a "beatification" miracle; this research found it is the canonization miracle instead, and found no evidence of a separate beatification-track miracle (none was needed — martyrs are dispensed from the beatification miracle entirely). This should be reconciled/corrected when the record is created — confirm this reading before publishing rather than assuming the priority-list note is simply wrong.
- **Recipient's exact age** is inconsistently reported across press sources (34, 35, and 36 all appear). Not resolved to a single authoritative figure.
- **Exact date of the medical crisis and discharge** (September 4 and September 10, 2015 respectively) comes from secondary press (Telemetro, El Mundo) summarizing what appears to be a 2018 Church press presentation of Flores's case, not from a primary/official document. Treated here at "month" precision for that reason; could be tightened to exact_day if a more authoritative source is found.
- **"Scientifically inexplicable" quote.** This phrase, widely repeated in English-language coverage of the March 2018 decree, was not found verbatim in the primary vatican.va/press.vatican.va bulletin text fetched during this research (which contains only the standard formulaic language naming the beatus, with no medical-board language at all). It may be a standard translation convention for the Latin/Italian decree formula used across many such decrees, or it may be from a Vatican press conference/briefing not captured in the bulletin itself. Needs a primary citation before being used as a direct quote on the site.
- **Consulta Medica proceedings are not published**, consistent with the Vatican's practice for every canonization miracle (see `context/Notes/Source Requirements Standard.md`) — this is a permanent structural gap, not something to keep chasing.
- **Hospital name/location precision.** Press accounts name both a "Hospital de Maternidad" (site of the cesarean, now renamed Hospital Nacional de la Mujer "Dra. María Isabel Rodríguez," ~13.6907/-89.2044) and the ISSS general hospital (where she was placed in an induced coma and recovered, ~13.7032/-89.2037) — two different facilities in San Salvador involved at different stages of treatment. The coordinates above (updated 2026-10-02 via OSM/Nominatim) now point to Hospital General ISSS, since that's where the organ-failure crisis and recovery took place; the original stored value had been a city-center fallback that coincidentally matched the cathedral's coordinates rather than either hospital.
- Her husband's name is consistently given as Alejandro Rivas across sources; her son born during the crisis is named Luis Carlos; one source additionally names two older children (Emiliano and Rebeca) — not independently cross-checked.
